We are giving away about a sixth of every website project.

A delivery process that makes scope visible, work shareable, and change chargeable.

Proposal to the management team  ·  Web delivery

Where we actually are

Six questions we cannot currently answer.

  1. How many hours did our last website project actually take?
  2. What did we estimate it at before we started?
  3. How many rounds of revisions did we give away?
  4. How much unbilled work did we absorb, and on what?
  5. What margin did we make on it?
  6. Which kind of project is most profitable for us?

We are a business that sells time, and we do not measure the time. Every figure in this deck is therefore an estimate — not because the numbers are being hidden, but because they do not exist yet.

That is the first finding, and it is the cheapest one to fix.

The pattern we all recognise

None of this is anyone’s fault. That is the problem.

  • Design starts before anyone agreed the structure
  • The client adds a page in week six and we absorb it
  • Nobody can prove it wasn’t in scope
  • One person holds the whole project in their head
  • Content arrives late and launch week becomes a scramble
What we think it costs

Our best guess at where the time goes.

Roughly per project — about of an average job, before anyone has made a mistake.

We could be wrong about all of it. Nobody can currently prove otherwise — which is the argument, not a weakness in it.

Why it happens

Three things are missing, and they cause all of it.

No baseline

We never write down what the project was estimated to take. So when it takes longer, there is nothing to compare against — and scope creep becomes a feeling rather than a fact.

No artefact to point at

Approval happens in conversations and Basecamp comments. When a client says “we always assumed that was included”, we have nothing to show them.

No gate to stop at

Work flows forward on assumption. Design starts before structure is agreed, and build starts before design is settled, so every late change unwinds three stages of work.

This is a structure problem, not a people problem. Asking the team to be more careful will not fix it.

The change, in one line

Stop starting with “design a website”.

Start with what the business needs the site to do, what pages serve that, what content those pages need, and which reusable blocks assemble them.

Each stage narrows uncertainty before the next one begins. Design becomes the application of a system to a known structure, rather than the place where the project is figured out.

The order of play

Twenty work packages. Five phases.

The order is deliberate. Sitemap before outlines, outlines before wireframes, blocks before design, spec before build. Every one of those orderings exists because reversing it causes rework we have actually had.

Gates

“Looks good” is not approval.

What we do now

  • Approval happens in a call or a Basecamp comment
  • Nobody records which version was approved
  • Work continues on the assumption it was fine
  • When it is disputed, it is one memory against another

What a gate is

  • Names the artefacts being approved, with version numbers
  • Names the person approving, and when
  • States exactly what work is now allowed to start
  • States what happens if it is not approved

A gate is one Basecamp ticket. The whole overhead of this process is about per project — and that is the figure the calculator subtracts before showing any return.

If the real overhead turns out to be double that, the trial will show it. That is what the trial is for.

Governance, cheaply

Two records that do different jobs.

Decision log — protects reasoning

Why something was chosen. Options considered, what we picked, the rationale, the reversal cost. It stops the same argument being had three times, and it means a new person can understand the project without asking anyone.

Change log — protects scope

What moved after approval. What changed, why, which approved artefact it affects, the effort it adds, and whether it is accepted, rejected or deferred.

Neither takes more than a minute to fill in. Both are the evidence that makes the next slide possible.

The commercial mechanism

Scope creep stops being an argument.

1. Classify

Every request is a correction (departs from what was approved — we fix it free), a preference (opinion within what we left open — absorbed in the revision allowance), or new scope.

2. Cost it

New scope gets an hours figure. Not “some impact”. Hours.

3. Offer it

If chargeable it becomes a variation order before the work happens. The client accepts, declines, or defers it. All three outcomes are fine.

We can still choose to absorb something. The process does not require charging for everything — it requires the decision to be visible. Absorbing twenty minutes as goodwill is a choice. Absorbing twenty hours because nobody was counting is not.

The obvious objection

“This is far too much for a five-page site.”

Correct. So it scales — three routes, and steps are omitted deliberately rather than forgotten.

Light is the only route that drops steps. Standard and full run the same ones — what changes is artefact depth, not the number of stages. And an omission is recorded with its reason, so it is a decision we can defend rather than something forgotten.

Capacity

Right now, a second person on a project costs us more than they add.

Today

  • Head of Web specifies the work and builds it
  • Project knowledge lives in one person’s head
  • Bringing a developer in means a walkthrough, so we often do not bother
  • The project takes as long as one person takes
  • Holiday or illness stalls the whole job

With work packages

  • Head of Web specifies and assures; a Web Developer delivers
  • Every package states its inputs, deliverables and done-when conditions
  • A block can be handed over with no project history required
  • Two people work in parallel without colliding
  • Nobody signs off their own work

Head of Web specifies the work. A Web Developer delivers it. Head of Web assures it at QA. Three jobs, not one person’s bottleneck.

The client side

The same work, worth more.

Perceived value

A discovery summary, a sitemap and a set of page outlines feel like consultancy. The same thinking delivered verbally feels like nothing was delivered at all.

Confidence

Clients relax when they can see what happens next and what they are agreeing to. Uncertainty is what makes them anxious, and anxious clients generate revision rounds.

Transparency

Gates mean they always know where the project is. No more “any update?” emails, because the answer is already visible.

Fewer surprises, both ways

They are not surprised by an invoice. We are not surprised by a requirement. Both of those relationships are cheaper to run.

Beyond the money

The week becomes predictable.

Less held in one head

Project state lives in artefacts rather than memory. Someone can be off for a week without the project stopping.

Fewer emergency reshuffles

Most fire-fighting comes from work starting before its inputs existed. Entry criteria remove that category of emergency.

Faster onboarding

New starters learn the process from the guide rather than by shadowing. That is a real recruitment and scaling constraint removed.

Estimating improves

Every project records baseline against actual. After three projects we know where we are systematically wrong — permanently.

Gates, logs and artefacts are exactly what a procurement questionnaire asks for.

To be clear

What this is not.

Not more meetings

A gate is a Basecamp ticket, not a meeting. The process removes status calls rather than adding them.

Not more admin for its own sake

About eight hours per project of artefacts, against an estimated twenty-plus recovered. If that ratio turns out to be wrong, we should stop.

Not slower

It front-loads decisions that we currently make late and expensively. Projects finish sooner because they unwind less.

Not the same for every project

Three routes, and a tool that decides which steps a given project genuinely needs.

Not a way to squeeze clients

It makes what we already do visible, so clients can see what they are buying. Most will value that.

Not finished

The effort figures are uncalibrated guesses until we run it three times. That is the point of the trial.

The ask

Start measuring. Then decide.

  1. Measure. Record the estimate and the actual on the next projects. We have never had this number.
  2. Track revision rounds given away, and variations raised and accepted.
  3. Run those projects through the process, whatever their size.
  4. Review after . If the overhead exceeds the recovery, we stop — and we will finally know.
  5. Name an owner today. Without one, this becomes a document nobody opens.

The guide, the templates and the scoping tool are already built. There is nothing to fund and nothing to wait for.
