Why we work this way

Three things every project needs, that nothing gives you for free.

A shared understanding

What the project is trying to do, agreed once, written down — so design, build and QA are all working from the same picture instead of reconstructing it from memory.

An artefact to point at

Every stage produces something named and versioned. When there's a question about what was agreed, there's something to open, not just someone to ask.

A gate to stop at

Work does not carry on into the next stage on assumption. Each gate is a deliberate point to confirm what's approved before building on top of it.

Everything that follows is those three things, applied twenty times over.

The shape of it

Five phases, each narrowing what's still undecided.

Each phase's output is the next phase's input. Nothing starts before the phase before it has been approved.

Before the detail — two words, two jobs

A step is work. A gate is a check.

Step (= work package)

  • One unit of work — someone does it
  • Has a defined input and a defined output
  • Twenty in the catalogue — thirteen to twenty run, depending on route
  • e.g. “Discovery and requirements capture”

Gate

  • A checkpoint after certain steps, not a step itself
  • Names what's approved, who approved it, what it unlocks
  • Eleven to fifteen of them run, depending on route
  • e.g. “Gate 1: Discovery complete”

Most steps end at a gate. A few don't — development is the clearest case: nobody approves “has building happened”, they approve what it produced, at QA. On the next slide, green means the step ends at a gate. Red means it doesn't.

Approval, made concrete

A gate names an artefact, a version, and a person.

Not a gate

  • “Looks good” in a call or a comment
  • Nobody records which version was approved
  • Work continues on the assumption it was fine

A gate

  • Names the artefacts being approved, with version numbers
  • Names the approver, and when
  • States exactly what work is now allowed to start
  • States what happens if it is not approved — the fail path

sequential gates run on every project, whatever the route. On Standard and Full, run in total. more are conditional — triggered by what a specific project actually involves, not by its size.

Scaling to the project

Three routes. Only one of them drops steps.

Standard and full run the same steps. Full outlines every page rather than every template, specs every block rather than just the new ones, and formalises change control on every item — not a longer list, a deeper one. The spec builder decides the route for you from a fifteen-question intake.

The counts above are each route's baseline — further steps and further gates are conditional, and run on any route when a project triggers them. That's next.

Risk, not size

What pulls extra work into a project.

Twelve conditions, set at discovery from what a project actually involves. Seven of them can also trigger a conditional gate — on any route, including light.

When something changes

Every request gets classified before anyone acts on it.

Correction

Departs from what was approved. Fixed, no charge.

Preference

An opinion within what was left open. Absorbed within the revision allowance.

New scope

Wasn't in the approved artefacts. Logged in the change log, costed in hours, offered as a variation order before the work happens.

The decision log and change log are the two records behind this: one protects why something was chosen, the other protects what moved after approval. Both are mandatory on every route, including light.
